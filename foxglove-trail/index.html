Foxglove Trail — for grown‑ups

A quiet map‑reading game for roughly ages 7–10. A round is three short nights and takes under two minutes. There is no way to lose.

Controls

  • Touch and hold anywhere — the fox runs towards your finger for as long as you hold.
  • Tap the fox (or just beside it, in the middle of the screen) — sniff, which pulses your position onto the map.
  • Tap anywhere on a title or between‑nights screen to carry on.
  • Grown‑ups and All games are the two buttons along the bottom.

The game fills the whole screen once you touch it, and the browser bars go away.

Those are the only keys the game uses. No mouse, anywhere.

What you do

A fox has to get home to the den before the evening fog closes in. You can only see a little way through the trees — but a paper map in the corner shows the whole wood, with the den marked by a dotted ring. The map is always drawn the same way up as the screen, so “the den is up‑left on the paper” means “run up‑left from here.” Landmarks — log, standing stone, berry bush, mushroom ring, pond — are drawn both in the wood and on the map, so a child can match them up. Pink foxgloves are optional detours.

The skill it builds

  • Allocentric → egocentric translation. Holding a bird's‑eye model of a place in mind while moving through it at ground level — the real skill behind map reading, orienteering and finding your way in an unfamiliar building.
  • Route choice under branching. The trail system grows more tangled as they improve, so more of the work is picking the branch that keeps heading home.
  • Landmark‑based wayfinding. Matching a symbol on paper to an object in front of you. Every junction carries a landmark, so it always pays off.
  • Route planning and self‑location. Sniffing is limited, so they keep a running sense of where they are rather than checking constantly.
  • Spatial working memory. The map is studied before the night starts and stays small during it, so part of the layout is carried in the head.

How it adapts, and how it ends

The game measures how directly each night is walked and keeps a rolling estimate of skill in this browser. Difficulty changes only one thing: how complicated the trail system is — a wider wood, more junctions, more tempting wrong turns. The map never rotates. Struggling brings the scaffolding back — a wider circle of sight, a faster‑recharging sniff, and a breadcrumb trail on the paper. Doing well removes those one at a time. There is no failure state: if the fog closes in completely, an owl calls from the den — you can hear which side it is on — and a soft glow points home. The night still finishes.
