Owlet Grove

A 60-second flight through the forest. Nothing to lose, nothing to crash into — just leaves, berries and fireflies to catch.

How to play

Press Space to flapEach press lifts Pip; gravity does the rest. Find a rhythm to climb, pause to glide down.
Press Q for this menuIt pauses the round. Press it again (or Resume) and play carries on from exactly where it stopped.
One round is 60 secondsShort on purpose — it fits a young attention span, and the clock is the only thing that ever runs out.

What things are worth

Green leaf — 1 pointCommon, and arranged in easy arcs that follow a natural flapping line.
Red berries — 2 pointsWorth a small detour off the easy line.
Glowing firefly — 5 pointsRare, brightest thing on screen, and deliberately placed off the easy line. Going for one usually means giving up two or three leaves — that trade is the whole game.

What it builds

Hand–eye coordination
Flapping is a timing skill: the wing beat has to land at the right instant, not just the right place. Repeating that a few hundred times a round trains the eye-to-finger loop that also drives handwriting and catching.
Trajectory calculation
Pip is always falling, so a flap has to be aimed at where the owl will be, not where it is. Children learn to read a curved path forward in time and intercept it — the same prediction used to catch a ball.
Risk & reward decisions
A firefly high up is worth five; the three leaves below are worth three and are far easier. Items arrive faster than they can be agonised over, so the child practises deciding quickly and committing.
There is no way to lose. Pip cannot crash, cannot be hurt and never gets a “Game Over” — brushing the treetops or the grass is just a soft bounce. When 60 seconds are up the score appears and one press starts a fresh flight.
